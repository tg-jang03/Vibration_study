# 6-2 Spectrum: read the path of a line, not just one line

During a start-up, a line below 1X has grown large. At first it moved along with the shaft speed, but now the machine runs faster and that line stays at the same frequency. What is this line telling you? If you had kept only one spectrum, could you have reached the same judgment?

This time you read the <a href={withBase('/en/p5-2/')}>waterfall and cascade built in P5-2</a> as a reading procedure. With <a href={withBase('/en/p5-3/')}>P5-3 Full spectrum</a> you will also separate the forward and backward components. Rather than learning how to build them again, decide what to check first in the same record.

<Callout type="idea" title="After reading this page">

1. You can check the shaft speed and read a spectrum starting from 1X.
2. You can tell order tracking, a fixed frequency, and tracking followed by locking apart.
3. You can calculate why the order goes down even though the Hz stays constant.
4. You can explain why records held at constant speed must be checked in time order.
5. You can tell the forward and backward radii of a full spectrum from the peak of a single channel.

</Callout>

The figures and the lab are a learning example made by synthesizing constant-speed 2-second records separately. They do not predict the onset limit or the amplitude of a real oil-film instability. Check first that the quantity is displacement and whether a value is a peak or a circle radius. Every component is placed exactly at the center of a 0.5 Hz bin so that the Hann amplitude comes out exactly. In a real record, a line that falls between bins can read up to about 15 % low (P2-5).

## 1. Read a spectrum from 1X, in six steps

Is it fine to put a cause's name on the largest line first? First check the actual shaft speed. At 7200 rpm the shaft makes 120 turns a second, so 1X is 120 Hz.

<Figure {...F.reading} />

Figure 1 is the displacement spectrum of one channel, X. Once you fix the position of 1X, you can compare how many times 1X the other lines are, whether they are below it, and whether they miss the integer multiples.

| Step | What you read in this example | What to check next |
|---|---|---|
| ① 1X | 120 Hz · 20 µm Peak | the actual shaft speed, other directions, phase, operating conditions |
| ② Harmonics | 2X = 240 Hz · 5 µm Peak | how many multiples continue, repetition and distortion of the waveform |
| ③ Below 1X | 40 Hz = 0.333X · 12 µm Peak | whether it moves as the shaft speed changes |
| ④ Asynchronous | 73 Hz · 3 µm Peak | another reference: a separate rotor, the power line, a fixed excitation |
| ⑤ High frequency | 320 Hz · 2 µm Peak | element frequencies, measurement band, filters, installation resonance |
| ⑥ Floor | 140 ~ 180 Hz zoom after adding noise | band, window, number of averages, conditions before and after the measurement |

③ and ④ are not completely separate classes. An asynchronous component can also be below 1X. The reading order is not a procedure that sorts the cause of a line into one answer box; it is a checklist that reduces the ranges you miss.

<Callout type="term" title="Subsynchronous and asynchronous are not the same word">

A subsynchronous component is one with a frequency lower than 1X. An asynchronous component is one that is not synchronized with the shaft rotation. For example, a stable repeating 0.5X and a separate 73 Hz excitation may relate to the shaft speed differently. Read the position of the line together with its relation to time and shaft speed.

</Callout>

Use <a href={withBase('/en/p1-8/')}>P1-8 Element frequencies</a> to find the places of interest and <a href={withBase('/en/p2-7/')}>P2-7 Units and scaling</a> to check the vertical axis. The high-frequency line in this example is a plain sinusoid; it does not stand in for a real impact band or an envelope result.

## 2. A line that looks the same in one plot behaves differently across records

There is a big line at 120 Hz and a small one at 40 Hz. Does each follow the shaft speed, or does it stay at the same Hz? Stack the earlier and later records to check.

<Figure {...F.orderLines} />

In Figure 2, follow the horizontal position of the spectrum from the bottom record to the top record. 1X and 2X move to the right as the shaft speed rises. 73 Hz stays at the same horizontal position throughout. The line below 1X is a path that first moves and then stays at 40 Hz.

As in <a href={withBase('/en/p5-2/')}>P5-2 §4</a>, a waterfall is stacked by recording time and a cascade by recorded shaft speed. In the stacked figures on this page, the displayed amplitude height is added to each baseline. Do not read the vertical coordinate of a peak top as the actual shaft speed or time. Read the amplitude values from the spectrum of the selected record.

<Callout type="warn" title="Match the comparison conditions before stacking records">

If the sensor, direction, unit, band, window or record length differ, the size of a line and the floor can also differ. A missing keyphasor or a wrong speed estimate changes the order reading. Confirm that the records come from the same operating range, and also read the time and speed order shown on the screen.

</Callout>

An order line follows $f=o f_r$. $f_r$ is the rotating frequency and $o$ is the order. If the order is constant, the Hz is proportional to the shaft speed. A fixed-Hz line, by contrast, stays at the same horizontal position even when the shaft speed changes.

At one speed, 73 Hz may happen to lie near some order. Do not conclude from the division result of one record that it is a shaft-speed-tracking (constant-order) component; check whether the relation continues at other speeds too.

## 3. The path from wheel-type tracking to whip-type locking

A line below 1X moved and then stopped. How does it differ from a line that was fixed from the start?

<Figure {...F.transition} />

Recall the frequency relations from <a href={withBase('/en/p4-4/')}>P4-4 Wheel and whip</a>. Oil whirl and oil whip are both fluid-induced instabilities. Whirl produces a subsynchronous component that follows a fraction of the shaft speed, and whip locks near the natural frequency of the rotor.

In Figure 3, blue is tracking only, orange is tracking followed by locking, and green is fixed from the start. A line that follows the speed and then stays at a known mode frequency, like the orange one, strongly supports the whip hypothesis. You still need other evidence — the bearing, operating conditions, orbit direction — before judging the real cause.

The locking path in the lab follows this simple frequency law.

$$f_{\rm sub}=\min(q f_r, f_n)$$

$q$ is the learning tracking ratio (default 0.45) and $f_n$ is the learning mode frequency (default 40 Hz). This is not the condition or the boundary equation of a real instability, and the sub amplitude of 12 µm in this example is also simply chosen.

<Callout type="example" title="Calculating the model crossing speed">

1. The condition where the tracking line meets the mode frequency is $q f_r=f_n$.
2. $f_r=40/0.45\approx88.89$ Hz.
3. In rpm, $N=60f_r\approx5333$ rpm.

If you change the mode to 50 Hz, it is $60\times50/0.45\approx6667$ rpm. This value is the model crossing point in the figure, not a safe speed of any machine or the speed where real whip starts. Figures 3 and 4 connect only records spaced 600 rpm apart, so the kink of the 40 Hz model (about 5333 rpm) shows up at the next record, 5400 rpm.

</Callout>

<Callout type="field" title="'How it got there' says more than 'the last 40 Hz'">

An excitation fixed at 40 Hz from the start sits at the same place as the locking model in the last record. Look together at the known natural frequency, the change in shaft speed, the bearing condition and the other channels. Do not confirm whip from one fixed frequency alone. Telling faults apart continues in Part 7.

</Callout>

The fixed 40 Hz reference line is not subsynchronous from the very start. At 1200 rpm in the first record, 1X is 20 Hz, so this line coincides with 2X. After the shaft speed rises, it comes below 1X. The same line changes its relative position.

A real natural frequency can change with operating conditions. This lab is a simple example with a constant $f_n$. Even when a line moves a little with load or temperature, do not finish the judgment with only "is it perfectly horizontal?"

## 4. The Hz is constant, so why does the order go down?

Seen on the order axis, the locked line comes down. Has the vibration frequency become lower?

<Figure {...F.orders} />

The order is the value divided by the actual rotating frequency of each record.

$$o_{\rm sub}=\frac{f_{\rm sub}}{f_r},\qquad f_r=\frac{N}{60}$$

$o_{\rm sub}$ is the order of the sub component and $N$ is in rpm. At 6000 rpm it is $40/100=0.4$X, and at 7200 rpm $40/120\approx0.333$X. The 40 Hz stays the same, but the denominator has grown.

The 0.45X line that only tracks goes up in the Hz plot (Figure 3), moves to the right in the cascade of Figure 2, and sits at the same place in the order plot. After locking, the opposite relation appears. Checking on the two axes, Hz and order, makes "tracking" and "locking" easy to explain.

<Callout type="warn" title="Changing the order axis does not create more information">

It only divides the frequency coordinate of the same FFT record by the shaft speed. This lab uses constant-speed records, so that conversion is possible. In a real fast run-up where the speed changes within the window, smearing appears, and dividing the coordinate alone does not solve it. Recall <a href={withBase('/en/p5-4/')}>P5-4 Order tracking</a>.

</Callout>

## 5. If it changes at the same speed, you return to the time axis

The speed stays at 7200 rpm, but the vibration has grown. From the cascade alone, can you tell when it changed?

<Figure {...F.hold} />

Records 10, 11 and 12 are at times 100, 110 and 120 s, all at 7200 rpm. X 1X goes 20 → 25 → 30 µm Peak. The waterfall separates the time baselines, but the speed baselines of the cascade overlap. On a screen where the three lines are overlaid, the order of the records can be hidden.

<Callout type="field" title="Check start-up and shutdown separately from constant-speed segments">

In start-up and shutdown, check the relation between the lines and the shaft speed; in constant-speed segments, check the relation with time and with load and temperature. If you drop the records of the holding segment or merge them into one rpm, the time of the change can disappear. As in <a href={withBase('/en/p6-4/')}>P6-4 Trend · APHT</a>, return to the time trend and the selected spectrum.

</Callout>

In this example the final amplitude increase is a record that was set by hand. Real causes of an increase — thermal bow, load change, sensor problems — are not reproduced here. Record down to "which component changed, and when", and tie the cause to separate evidence.

## 6. A full spectrum cascade follows the forward and backward components separately

The 1X size of the X channel is the same, yet can the whirl-direction components differ? Check with the two simultaneously measured channels, X and Y.

<Figure {...F.full} />

In Figure 6, both +1X and −1X follow the shaft speed. The component below 1X tracks on the + side and then locks at 40 Hz. The spectrum of a single channel cannot separate the forward and backward components of the same frequency like this.

<Callout type="term" title="On this page, +f is counterclockwise and −f is clockwise">

As in the <a href={withBase('/en/p5-3/')}>P5-3 convention</a>, X points right, Y points up, and $z=x+jy$ is computed. The shaft is also assumed to turn counterclockwise, so +f is forward and −f is backward. If the real shaft turns clockwise, the forward and backward names swap. A negative frequency is the direction label of this decomposition; it does not mean time runs backwards.

</Callout>

<Figure {...F.split} />

In Figure 7 the 1X radii are 15 µm on the + side and 5 µm on the − side. In this example the phase references of the two circular components are matched so that the X amplitude is their sum, 20 µm Peak. This does not mean that the X amplitude can in general be calculated as the simple sum of the two radii.

If you change the backward share to 50 %, + and − are 10/10 µm and X 1X stays at 20 µm Peak. Even such a straight-line motion has equal sizes on both sides when split into forward and backward circular components. Having a backward component does not always mean rub or instability.

<Callout type="warn" title="Check the sensor conditions before reading the direction components">

Check the simultaneity, sensitivity, angle, polarity, axis direction and phase convention of X/Y. Sensitivity or angle errors in the sensors can create a false backward component (P5-3). Also consider anisotropic response and runout, and connect to the orbit, the time waveform, the phase and the operating records. Do not confirm a fault from a ± bar alone.

</Callout>

## 7. Lab: read the same records on three axes

Choose the records of Figures 1 ~ 7 yourself. Rather than reading the amplitude from the vertical height of the stacked figure, look at the selected spectrum below and the readouts together.

<Callout type="try">

1. With the initial lock, 40 Hz and ratio 0.45, change the selected record from 6 (4800 rpm) to 10 (7200 rpm). Write down the Hz and order of the sub component, then change the scenario to tracking only and compare the last line.
2. Go back to locking and change the mode to 50 Hz. Calculate the model crossing speed and compare it with the readout. Then, at 40 Hz again, pick the fixed-from-the-start scenario and look at the earlier records too.
3. Return to the initial lock, then pick records 10 → 11 → 12 in the waterfall. Check the change in the size of 1X at the same rpm, then switch to the cascade and look at how the baselines overlap.
4. In record 10 with the full cascade, change the backward share from 25 to 50 %. Compare X 1X with the ±1X radii, and also check Hz → order axis and adding noise.

</Callout>

<WaterfallLab client:visible lang="en" />

In the lock of item 1 it is 36 → 40 Hz and 0.45 → 0.333X; with tracking only, the last record is 54 Hz and 0.45X. The model crossing point in item 2 is about 6667 rpm. A 40 Hz line fixed from the start and a line locked after tracking have different histories even if their last records are the same.

In item 3, with the speed held at 7200 rpm, X 1X goes 20 → 25 → 30 µm Peak. You only separate the records by looking in time order. In item 4, X 1X stays at 20, yet the +/− radii change from 15/5 to 10/10 µm. Write down the forward/backward phase condition of this example together with the sensor axis convention.

If you pick record 2 (2400 rpm) in fixed-from-the-start with 40 Hz, both the specified sub component and 1X are at 40 Hz. That bin of X adds up to 20+12=32 µm Peak. In this example the two components have the same phase, so they add simply; in general the value lies between 8 and 32 µm depending on the phase. A line in the FFT is a combined response, not a list split by cause name.

## 8. Summary: first the position, then the movement, last the other evidence

| Question you read | What you see | What it hides or what to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Where is 1X? | the actual shaft speed and the relative position of components | keyphasor or speed errors, Peak/RMS, units |
| Does it follow the order? | Hz moving in proportion to shaft speed | it may coincide by chance at one speed |
| Does it lock to a Hz? | staying near the natural frequency after tracking | tell it apart from an excitation fixed from the start |
| Does it change at the same rpm? | component changes along the time axis | overlapping cascade baselines |
| Which of forward and backward changes? | direction components of X/Y, speed dependence | sensors, polarity, rotation direction, radius notation |
| Did a line or the floor grow? | the change in that record and band | check settings, leakage, other components, other channels |

## 9. Check questions

<details>
<summary><strong>Q1. At 4800 rpm, how many X is the 36 Hz line?</strong></summary>

1X is $4800/60=80$ Hz, and $36/80=0.45$X. Do not confirm whirl from the ratio at one speed alone.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2. From 6000 to 7200 rpm the 40 Hz stays, yet it went down from 0.4 to 0.333X. Is that a contradiction?</strong></summary>

No. The frequency stayed the same and the rotating frequency grew from 100 to 120 Hz. The order is the value divided by the rotating frequency of each record.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3. The last spectrum shows 40 Hz. Is it whip?</strong></summary>

You cannot judge from one plot. Check the history of tracking and then locking near a known natural frequency, and the bearing, operating conditions, other channels and whirl direction. An excitation fixed from the start can sit at the same place.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4. The speed is the same, so can I merge records 10 ~ 12 into one?</strong></summary>

In this example X 1X changes 20 → 25 → 30 µm Peak, so you need the time information. Keep the waterfall, the time trend and the selected records along with the cascade.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q5. With +1X = 15 µm and −1X = 5 µm, can I report a fault?</strong></summary>

A backward component alone does not confirm a fault. Check whether they are forward and backward circle radii, and whether the shaft rotation direction and the sensor conditions match, and connect to other evidence. The X value of 20 µm Peak in this example is a specific combined condition with the phases of the two circular components matched.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q6. The neighboring bins of the 2-second Hann FFT also went up. Is it a new component?</strong></summary>

This example has a resolution of 0.5 Hz, and the neighbor of a 1X placed on a bin shows half the amplitude through the Hann main lobe. Do not assign a different cause to one neighboring bin; check the line width, record length and window (P2-4, P2-5).
</details>
