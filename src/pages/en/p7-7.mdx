# 7-7 Electrical causes: electrical or mechanical?

The horizontal vibration of a pump driven by a 2-pole induction motor has grown. The largest line in the spectrum is "near 120 Hz". The maintenance team says it is 2X, so let's recheck the alignment, and the electrical team says it is twice the line frequency (2×LF), so the problem is in the motor. The two candidates are less than 1 Hz apart, and what you would repair is completely different. Which is it?

In P2-4 we saw that separating 2X from 2×LF is a resolution problem, and in P7-1 how to work out the speed backwards from the synchronous speed and slip. This page sorts the **vibration made by magnetic forces** inside motors and generators by cause, and gets you used to two tools for separating electrical from mechanical: record length and the power-cut test.

<Callout type="idea" title="After reading this page you can">

1. Explain why magnetic force proportional to the square of the current becomes 2×LF, and why stator problems and static air-gap eccentricity increase 2×LF.
2. Calculate the synchronous speed, slip frequency and pole-pass frequency (PPF) of an induction motor, and know that 2×LF lies above the pole-number harmonic by exactly the PPF.
3. Read the PPF sidebands of a rotor bar fault and dynamic air-gap eccentricity by choosing the record length.
4. Separate electrical from mechanical with the power-cut test (for a generator, a field cut while keeping the speed).
5. Know the pitfalls: 1X = LF of a 2-pole synchronous generator, inverter drives, and shaft voltage and bearing electrical damage.

</Callout>

## 1. Why magnetic force is 2×LF: the square of the current

A motor or generator turns the iron core into a magnet with the current in its coils, and either the pull of that magnet turns the rotor (motor) or the rotor turns and makes current (generator). The pull acts across the narrow gap between the stator and the rotor, the air gap (P1-6).

<Figure {...F.forceDoubling} />

The force with which a magnet pulls iron is proportional to the **square** of the magnetic flux (the strength of the magnetic field), and the flux is proportional to the current. So the iron is pulled equally whether the current flows positive or negative. As in Figure 1, a 60 Hz current has one + peak and one − peak per cycle (16.7 ms), and the pull is strong at both, so it surges every 8.33 ms, that is, at <strong>120 Hz (2×LF)</strong>. In an equation, $\cos^2(2\pi f_L t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[1 + \cos(2\pi\, 2f_L\, t)\right]$ — a constant pull on average (0 Hz) and a surge at $2f_L$.

This frequency **follows only the line frequency and is independent of speed**. Even if the load changes and the speed moves by a few rpm, 2×LF stays at 120 Hz (100 Hz on a 50 Hz grid). This is why electrical causes sat at "fixed positions independent of speed" on the P7-1 map.

## 2. When the air gap is uneven: stator problems and static air-gap eccentricity

<Figure {...F.airGap} />

If the air gap is even around the circumference, the rotor is pulled equally in all directions and the resultant is 0 (Figure 2, left). Even so, the stator core is squeezed and released by that pull at 2×LF, so even a healthy motor has a small 2×LF. If the gap is narrow on one side only, the story changes. The flux is denser on the narrow side and pulls harder, and the rotor receives a **2×LF resultant force toward the narrow side** (middle).

<Callout type="term" title="Air-gap eccentricity">

A state in which the gap between the rotor and the inner surface of the stator is uneven around the circumference. When the inside of the stator is deformed or the rotor is shifted to one side so that **the narrow side stays at a fixed place**, it is called static eccentricity; when the rotor itself is bent or its outside is off the rotation center so that **the narrow side turns with the rotor**, it is called dynamic eccentricity.

</Callout>

The causes that increase 2×LF cluster on the stator side: static air-gap eccentricity (worn bearing housings, a misaligned center between stator and bearings), a loose stator core or winding, a core with damaged insulation between laminations, and problems with the fixing bolts that twist the frame. The evidence looks like this.

- **Frequency**: exactly 2×LF. It does not move when the speed changes.
- **Amplitude**: varies a little with voltage and current (load). There is no rule such as the square of the speed.
- **Phase**: unrelated to 1X (not tied to the keyphasor).
- **Direction**: radial. For static eccentricity, it is large in the direction of the narrow side.
- **Operating condition**: disappears the moment the power is cut (§5).

<Callout type="field" title="A motor with a lifted foot also increases 2×LF">

If a soft foot, where one of the four feet does not touch the base, is tightened by its bolt, the frame twists and the inside of the stator is deformed, becoming static eccentricity. 2×LF grows then, but the root is installation, not electricity. The frequency tells you only the **kind of force** (magnetic), not the cause that enlarged that force. The way to confirm is to loosen the bolts one by one and see whether 2×LF decreases.

</Callout>

## 3. Frequencies of the induction motor: slip and pole-pass frequency

In P7-1 §4.3 we said that an induction motor turns slower than the synchronous speed by the slip. The magnetic field turns at the synchronous speed and the rotor lags slightly behind it, so **seen from the rotor, the field slowly overtakes it at the slip frequency**. The field has $P$ N and S poles, so a point on the rotor passes a pole $P \times f_{slip}$ times per second. This is the pole-pass frequency (PPF).

$$
f_{sync} = \frac{2 f_L}{P}, \qquad f_{slip} = f_{sync} - f_r, \qquad \mathrm{PPF} = P\, f_{slip} = 2 f_L - P\, f_r
$$

- $f_L$: line frequency, $P$: number of poles, $f_r$: rotation frequency (1X), $f_{sync}$: synchronous speed [Hz]

<Callout type="example" title="2-pole 60 Hz motor, load 60 % (nameplate 3560 rpm)">

1. Synchronous speed: $2 \times 60 / 2 = 60$ Hz (3600 rpm). Full-load slip 1.11 %, so at 60 % load the slip is 0.67 %
2. Speed: 3600 × (1 − 0.0067) = **3576 rpm**, 1X = 59.6 Hz
3. Slip frequency: 60 − 59.6 = 0.4 Hz, PPF = 2 × 0.4 = **0.8 Hz**
4. 2X = 119.2 Hz, 2×LF = 120 Hz. Their difference of 0.8 Hz is exactly the PPF

</Callout>

<Figure {...F.inductionLadder} />

The last equation, $\mathrm{PPF} = 2f_L - P f_r$, is worth remembering. **2×LF always stands above the pole-number harmonic (P·X) by exactly the PPF** (Figure 3). For 2 poles, 2X is the neighbor of 2×LF, for 4 poles 4X, and when the two are mixed in one bin they beat (P2-8) at the PPF period. As the load increases, the slip grows and the PPF widens (1.33 Hz at full load for 2 poles).

<Callout type="warn" title="'The line next to 2×LF is always 2X'">

That is true only for 2-pole motors. A 4-pole 50 Hz motor has a 1X of about 24.7 Hz, so 2X (49.4 Hz) is far from 100 Hz, and the line just below 100 Hz, at 98.8 Hz, is **4X** (Figure 3, bottom). Check the number of poles first, and look for P·X next to 2×LF.

</Callout>

## 4. Rotor bars and dynamic air-gap eccentricity: PPF sidebands around 1X

The rotor of an induction motor is a cage made of aluminum or copper bars (rotor bars) tied together by rings at both ends. If one bar cracks or its joint to the ring breaks, current cannot flow through that bar, and the magnetic force is weak only at that place on the rotor. This weak spot passes the poles at the slip frequency, so the size of the force pulling on the rotor **rises and falls at the PPF**. The size of 1X is modulated at the PPF (the AM of P2-8), so **1X ± PPF sidebands** stand on both sides of 1X.

<Figure {...F.rotorBar} />

The sidebands are only the PPF (here 0.8 Hz) away from 1X, so record length decides success. With a Hann window, the sidebands stand separately only when they are 3 bins or more from 1X, so the record needs $T \ge 3/\mathrm{PPF}$, that is, 3.75 s or more. In Figure 4, 2 s (1.6 bins) gives one lump and 8 s (6.4 bins) gives three lines. If the record cannot be lengthened, use a Zoom FFT (P2-4) to magnify only around 1X.

The rotor bar sidebands have other fingerprints as well.

- **They change with load**: the more current is unable to flow in the broken bar, the greater the asymmetry, so the greater the load, the larger the sidebands and the wider the PPF. They are hard to see at light load.
- **They also show in the current**: in the motor current spectrum, sidebands of the same cause stand at $\pm$PPF (= 2 × slip × LF) on both sides of the line frequency (motor current analysis). Looking at vibration and current together increases confidence.
- **Dynamic air-gap eccentricity**: the narrow side turns with the rotor, so the pull turns at 1X and is modulated at the PPF, and **2×LF ± PPF** stands along with 1X ± PPF (Figure 2, right). For 2 poles, 2×LF − PPF is the 2X position, so it can look as if 2X has grown.

<Callout type="warn" title="'1X is large and there are no sidebands, so it is unbalance'">

In a 2 s record spectrum, the 1X of a rotor bar fault is also a clean single line like unbalance (Figure 4, top). Before drawing a conclusion, measure again under load with a record longer than $3/\mathrm{PPF}$. Conversely, if the broken bar heats unevenly and the rotor bends (thermal bow, P7-2), 1X remains even when the power is cut, so do not rule out a rotor bar fault from the power-cut test result alone.

</Callout>

## 5. The decisive test: the moment the power is cut

Magnetic force exists only while there are current and flux, and mechanical forces (unbalance, misalignment) are made by rotation. When the power is cut, the flux usually disappears in well under a second, but the speed falls slowly over several seconds to several minutes because of inertia. The share that decreases in **that short interval** is electrical.

<Figure {...F.tripTest} />

Figure 5 is a 2-pole motor with both misalignment (2X) and a stator problem (2×LF). With a 0.5 s frame (Δf 2 Hz), the two lines are mixed in one bin and become stronger and weaker every 1.25 s (= 1/PPF). At the moment the power is cut, the 120 Hz position weakens abruptly, and the remaining 2X bends downward following the speed and is at 73 % of its initial value at 102.2 Hz after 0.5 s. The electrical component is already at 0.67 % by then.

- **How to record**: with transient acquisition (P3-5), recording without a break from before the cut, look at a spectrogram (P5-2) or at the trend of each component. Changes on a 0.1 s scale must be seen, so trend values at intervals of several seconds will not do.
- **What to look at**: the share that decreases within the first 0.5 s, while the speed is almost unchanged, is electrical, and the share that continues to decrease following the speed is mechanical.

<Callout type="warn" title="For a motor run by an inverter, 'LF' moves">

An inverter (VFD) controls the speed by changing the frequency of the power fed to the motor. Then LF becomes the inverter output frequency, and 2×LF also **moves with the speed**. This makes the judgment "it follows the speed, so it is mechanical" wrong, so write down the output frequency shown on the inverter along with the record and check that the line is twice that. This is also the reason why components at the inverter's switching frequency (several kHz) stand in the acceleration spectrum. The power-cut test can be used as it is.

</Callout>

<Callout type="warn" title="Do the test together with operations">

Cutting the power stops the process. Do it in line with the shutdown plan, or set the recorder running beforehand when switching over to the standby machine. If you record at every shutdown, you do not need a separate test.

</Callout>

## 6. Lab: electrical or mechanical?

<Callout type="try">

1. From the initial state (2-pole 60 Hz · stator · load 60 %), change the record length from 1 s to 8 s and look around 2×LF. Then, in the spectrogram below, read what disappears at t = 0 and the "size remaining 0.5 s after the test".
2. Change only the cause to misalignment and look at the same two things. What is different?
3. Change the cause to rotor bar and, with an 8 s record, raise the load 20 → 60 → 100 %. What are the spacing and size of the sidebands? How many seconds of record are needed to see them at 20 % load?
4. Change the view mode to "Identify the hidden cause" and tell cases 1 to 7 apart. The generator cases are a test that cuts the field while keeping the speed (§7).

</Callout>

<ElectricLab client:visible lang="en" />

In step 1, at 1 s (Δf 1 Hz) the PPF of 0.8 Hz is only 0.8 bin, so 119.2 Hz and 120 Hz are one lump. At 8 s (6.4 bins), 120 Hz is large at 3.2 mm/s and 119.2 Hz is only a little over 0.2 mm/s. The moment the power is cut, the 120 Hz line disappears and the size remaining after 0.5 s is 0.19. In step 2, the misalignment at 8 s has 119.2 Hz large at about 2.9 mm/s (the line lies between bins, so it reads a little lower than the actual 3.25), and even when the power is cut the 2X bends with the speed and remains, at 0.71 after 0.5 s and 0.35 after 2 s. In step 3, the PPF is 0.27 → 0.8 → 1.33 Hz and the sidebands are 0.1 → 0.3 → 0.5 mm/s. The 0.27 Hz at 20 % load is 2.1 bins at 8 s, so it is buried in the skirt of the 1X peak, and shows up at 16 s (4.3 bins) when seen on the dB axis. Step 4 is: 1 stator, 2 misalignment, 3 rotor bar (4-pole, load 80 %, 1X ± 1.6 Hz), 4 unbalance, 5 generator stator magnetic force, 6 generator mechanical 2X, 7 dynamic air-gap eccentricity.

## 7. The 2-pole synchronous generator: when the positions overlap completely

In a synchronous machine the rotor turns exactly with the magnetic field and there is no slip. So PPF = 0, and for a 2-pole 60 Hz generator, 1X = 60 Hz = LF and 2X = 120 Hz = 2×LF, so **the positions overlap exactly**. For a 4-pole generator (1800 rpm), 2×LF = 4X.

<Figure {...F.generator} />

An overlapped line cannot be separated no matter how long the record. Instead, use a test that changes only the magnetic force. If you disconnect the generator from the grid and, keeping the rated speed (no-load rated speed), reduce or cut the field current — the current of the rotor electromagnet — the magnetic force falls within a few seconds following the flux, while the mechanical component does not change because the speed is unchanged (Figure 6: after 2 s the stator side goes 3.4 → 0.46 and the mechanical side 3.38 → 3.26 mm/s).

<Callout type="field" title="A 1X that responds slowly to the field current">

After the field current is changed, the 1X vector sometimes turns slowly **over tens of minutes**. This is not magnetic force but a thermal phenomenon in which the field winding heats unevenly and the rotor bends (field thermal sensitivity). If it responds immediately, it is magnetic force; if slowly, it is thermal. It is covered in P8-5 together with the 2×LF core vibration of generators.

</Callout>

## 8. Shaft voltage and bearing electrical damage

Electricity does not only make the force of vibration; it can also damage bearings directly. A voltage arises between the shaft and the housing when the magnetic circuit is uneven, when static electricity builds up on the shaft from steam or belt friction, or when an inverter puts a voltage on the shaft. When this voltage punches through the bearing's oil film and discharges, small melted marks form on the races, and these pile up into washboard-like stripes (fluting).

- **In vibration**: high-frequency components and fault frequencies similar to a rolling bearing fault (P7-5) appear. From vibration alone it is hard to separate from ordinary fatigue damage.
- **Confirmation**: measure the shaft voltage, and look at the stripes on the surface of a removed bearing and the black discoloration of the lubricant.
- **Direction of countermeasures**: make a separate path for the current with a shaft grounding brush, or insulate one bearing. Shaft currents in generators and steam turbines are in P8-5.

## 9. A table for telling electrical from mechanical

| What you see | Electrical cause | Mechanical look-alike | How to separate |
|---|---|---|---|
| A large line near 2×LF | Stator problem · static air-gap eccentricity | 2X of a 2-pole machine (misalignment · looseness), 4X of a 4-pole machine | Separate the two lines with a record $T \ge 3/\mathrm{PPF}$, power cut |
| Narrow sidebands on both sides of 1X, 1X beating | Rotor bar · dynamic air-gap eccentricity (± PPF) | Beating with a neighboring machine at a slightly different speed | Whether the spacing = PPF, whether the spacing changes with load, current spectrum |
| ± PPF on both sides of 2×LF | Dynamic air-gap eccentricity | — | Sideband spacing, power cut |
| 1X · 2X of a synchronous machine | Stator magnetic force (2×LF), field heating (1X) | Unbalance · misalignment | Field current change or cut while keeping the speed, response speed |
| A rolling-bearing-fault-like pattern | Electrical damage from shaft current | Fatigue spalling | Shaft voltage, bearing surface |
| A "2×LF" that moves with the speed | Twice the inverter output frequency | Mechanical 2X | Check the inverter output frequency |

What can be separated with a single frequency plot is only the case where you know the number of poles and the slip and the record is long enough. The decisive evidence is **the response at the moment the power (field) is cut**.

## 10. Summary

| Topic | What to remember |
|---|---|
| 2×LF | Magnetic force ∝ current² → twice the line frequency, a fixed position independent of speed |
| Stator · static eccentricity | Narrow side fixed → a 2×LF resultant in one direction. Soft foot increases it too |
| Slip · PPF | $\mathrm{PPF} = P f_{slip} = 2f_L - P f_r$: 2×LF lies above P·X by the PPF, and it widens with load |
| Rotor bar · dynamic eccentricity | 1X ± PPF (dynamic eccentricity also 2×LF ± PPF). Measure under load with $T \ge 3/\mathrm{PPF}$ |
| Power-cut test | The share that disappears in the first moment, while the speed is almost unchanged = electrical; if it decreases following the speed = mechanical |
| Synchronous generator | 1X = LF, 2X = 2×LF overlap → change or cut the field while keeping the speed |
| Pitfalls | With an inverter, LF moves; shaft current looks like a bearing fault |

## 11. Review questions

<details>
<summary><strong>Q1. A 4-pole 60 Hz motor with a nameplate of 1770 rpm is now running at 1785 rpm. What are the slip frequency, the PPF, and the line just below 2×LF, in Hz and what is it?</strong></summary>

Synchronous speed = 2 × 60 / 4 = 30 Hz (1800 rpm), 1X = 1785/60 = 29.75 Hz. Slip frequency = 30 − 29.75 = **0.25 Hz**, PPF = 4 × 0.25 = **1.0 Hz**. Just below 2×LF = 120 Hz is not 2X (59.5 Hz) but **4X = 119.0 Hz**, and their difference of 1.0 Hz is the PPF.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2. A 2-pole 60 Hz motor (load 60 %, 3576 rpm) is measured with a 1 s record and a 3 mm/s line stands at 120 Hz. Can you report "it is 2×LF, so an electrical problem"?</strong></summary>

**Not yet.** 2X is 119.2 Hz, differing from 2×LF by the PPF = 0.8 Hz, but with Δf 1 Hz for a 1 s record that is 0.8 bin and the two are one lump. Lengthen the record to $3/0.8 \approx 3.75$ s or more (for example 4 to 8 s) to see which of the two lines is larger, and if possible record the moment of a power cut to check the share that disappears right away.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3. You recorded a power cut, and the component near 120 Hz went from 4.0 mm/s before the cut to 0.6 mm/s within 0.3 s, and after that decreased slowly following the speed. How do you interpret it?</strong></summary>

The share that disappeared in the first 0.3 s, while the speed was almost unchanged, is **electrical**, and the remaining 0.6 mm/s that decreases following the speed is the **mechanical component** (2X). Most (about 85 %) is electrical, so look first at the stator and air-gap eccentricity. The mechanical 2X is not zero either, so also keep a record of the alignment condition.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4. Suspecting a rotor bar fault, you looked at a 2 s record at 30 % load and there are no sidebands around 1X. Can you conclude the rotor bars are healthy?</strong></summary>

**No.** For 2-pole 60 Hz (nameplate 3560 rpm), the slip at 30 % load is 0.33 %, the slip frequency 0.2 Hz and the PPF 0.4 Hz. In a 2 s record that is 0.8 bin, so the sidebands are buried in 1X, and at light load the sidebands themselves are also small. Raise the load and record $3/0.4 = 7.5$ s or more (8 s or more), or look at the current spectrum together.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q5. In a 2-pole 60 Hz generator the 120 Hz component has grown. Even with the record lengthened to 32 s it is a single line. How do you separate it?</strong></summary>

In a synchronous machine 2X and 2×LF are **exactly the same 120 Hz**, so they cannot be separated by record length. Disconnect it from the grid and, keeping the speed at 3600 rpm, reduce or cut the field current. If it decreases along within a few seconds, it is magnetic force (the stator core side); if it stays, it is mechanical 2X (the alignment · coupling side).
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q6. For a pump motor run by an inverter, a report came in saying "the line that looked like 2×LF moved along when the speed was changed, so it is a mechanical 2X". Is that right?</strong></summary>

**It needs checking.** With an inverter drive, LF is the inverter output frequency, so when the speed is changed 2×LF moves along too. Record the inverter output frequency together and check whether the line is exactly twice the output frequency (electrical) or twice the speed (below it by the slip) (mechanical), and if possible look at the moment of a power cut.
</details>
