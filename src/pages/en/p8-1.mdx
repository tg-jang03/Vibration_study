# 8-1 Startup, shutdown, and crossing critical speeds

In a startup record, the 1X amplitude rises to a peak, falls, and then rises again at a higher speed. The neighboring bearing shows similar peaks, but the center sensor hardly sees the second one.

It is the same shaft. Which sensor should you trust? Let us extend the single-mode (one-degree-of-freedom) unbalance response from P4-1 to several modes and compare records with sensor position and thermal state in mind.

<Callout type="idea" title="After reading this page">

1. Explain why several shaft-system modes create several resonance regions during startup.
2. Read bearing peaks, phase, and Polar plots together, and distinguish in-phase and anti-phase motion from modal nodes.
3. Explain how modal superposition and heavy damping change peaks and phase transitions.
4. Read differences between cold run-up and hot coastdown, and the limits of slow roll compensation.
5. Distinguish AF (amplification factor) and SM (separation margin) from protection-system records, and choose what to check during a critical-speed crossing.

</Callout>

---

## 1. Two peaks: a shaft system has several modes too

In P1-5, a mode was a particular shape in which several locations move together. In a shaft system, the supports and shaft move together, and each mode has a different natural frequency. Synchronous resonance occurs in the speed range where the 1X excitation frequency meets a mode's natural frequency.

<Figure {...F.modeShapes} />

In the <strong>rigid-body modes</strong> in Figure 1, the shaft moves on its supports with almost no bending. In translation, both ends move in the same direction; in a conical mode, the shaft tilts and the ends move in opposite directions. Flexible supports can bring these modes into a low-speed range.

In a <strong>bending mode</strong>, the shaft itself bends. In the illustrated first bending mode, the center moves strongly; the second bending example has a node at the center, where displacement is zero. Actual shapes and the order of modes depend on the shaft, disks, bearings, and support conditions.

<Callout type="term" title="Modal superposition">

This method adds the displacement that each mode produces at a location to obtain the sensor response. Even at the same 1X frequency, each mode has a different magnitude and phase, so **the contributions are added as complex vectors**. It combines the mode shapes of P1-5 with the vector addition of P3-3.

</Callout>

The lab on this page is a linear steady-state example with two fixed modes and a damping ratio for each. In bearing 1, bearing 2, center order, the assigned shapes are [1, 1, 1.5] for mode 1 and [1, −1, 0] for mode 2. These values let us observe symmetric and antisymmetric responses; they do not represent the design shapes or critical speeds of an actual turbine.

<Callout type="warn" title="'Anti-phase bearings always mean second bending?'">

Both conical motion and antisymmetric bending can appear anti-phase at the ends. First check that the sensors point in the same direction and use the same Keyphasor reference. Then compare several measurement locations with a dynamic model to distinguish the modes.

</Callout>

## 2. Peaks and phase: modal contributions combine at the sensor

What does startup look like for a shaft with two modes? The example in Figure 2 has natural speeds of 1500 rpm for mode 1 and 3000 rpm for mode 2, with an operating speed of 3600 rpm.

<Figure {...F.twoPeaks} />

In Figure 2, the sampled peaks at bearing 1 are 1500 rpm·83.46 µm pp and 3020 rpm·78.73 µm pp. A natural speed and a measured peak do not necessarily coincide. As P4-1 showed, an unbalance response peaks slightly above the natural speed, and the vector of the other mode is added on top of that.

Multiply each modal response by the shape value at the sensor location, then add the results. First consider the cold case, where the shaft has no bow (a bent shaft, P7-2 §3).

$$
X_j(N)=\sum_{m=1}^{2}\Phi_{jm}
\frac{e_m r_m^2 e^{-j\theta_m}}{1-r_m^2+j2\zeta_m r_m},
\qquad r_m=\frac{N}{N_m}
$$

$X_j$ is the complex 1X displacement (Peak) at sensor location $j$, and $\Phi_{jm}$ is the signed mode-shape value there. $N_m$ and $\zeta_m$ are the natural speed and damping ratio of mode $m$; $e_m$ and $\theta_m$ are its modal unbalance magnitude and lag angle for the chosen shape normalization. Context distinguishes the imaginary unit $j=\sqrt{-1}$ from the sensor subscript $j$.

The displayed amplitude is p-p, $2\lvert X_j\rvert$, and the lag angle is $\varphi_j=-\arg X_j$. If the shape values are scaled arbitrarily, $e_m$ must also be changed accordingly to preserve the same physical response. The lab keeps the shape values fixed.

<Figure {...F.superposition} />

Adding the gray mode-1 and purple mode-2 amplitudes arithmetically in Figure 3 does not produce the blue curve. Between the two peaks, opposing vectors reduce the resultant amplitude, and the resultant phase can turn back quickly. Such a cancellation valley is a clue to the <strong>antiresonance</strong> seen in P5-3; phase measurements also become unstable at small amplitudes.

<Callout type="example" title="90° for one modal contribution differs from 90° for the whole sensor response">

Mode 2 alone is 75 µm pp·90° at 3000 rpm. At bearing 1, adding the tail of mode 1 (the mode-1 response that remains at speeds above its resonance) gives 77.21 µm pp·99.88°. At bearing 2, the sign of mode 2 reverses, giving 75.12 µm pp·259.84°.

</Callout>

A peak together with a phase transition in the same region strengthens the case for resonance. If there is a peak without a phase transition, investigate changes in excitation force and measurement effects. Conversely, heavy damping, a node, or modal cancellation can hide a clear peak even when resonance is present.

<Callout type="warn" title="'Measured phase must change by 180° for each mode?'">

An isolated mode has a phase transition of about 180° from low to high speed. A measurement is a combined response over a finite speed range, so it need not give exactly that value. Also distinguish a jump at the 0°/360° display boundary from an actual abrupt change (P6-4).

</Callout>

## 3. Polar plots and several sensors: read loops and nodes together

What clues become clearer when Bode amplitude and phase are combined into a vector path?

<Figure {...F.polarLoops} />

In the Polar plots of Figure 4, 0° is the sensor direction, and lag increases clockwise, opposite to the default counterclockwise rotation. The points are 1X vectors measured at different speeds by the same sensor. They do not show the X/Y positions traced by the shaft during one revolution, as the orbit in P6-3 does.

Well-separated modes produce a rounded path in each resonance region. However, the high-speed end approaches a nonzero unbalance response, and overlapping modes mix the paths. **Do not determine the number of modes by counting loops alone; align the speed markers with the Bode plots.**

<Figure {...F.nodeSensor} />

The center sensor in Figure 5 has a mode-2 shape value of 0. Its 19.94 µm pp response at 3000 rpm is the tail of mode 1, with no mode-2 contribution. Weak excitation of a mode can also make its peak hard to see.

The phase difference between the two bearings at the same speed is about 2.74° at 1500 rpm and about 160° at 3000 rpm. These are clues to the symmetric first and antisymmetric second shapes, but the other mode mixes in, so the differences are not exactly 0° and 180°.

<Callout type="try">

2. Change 'Sensors shown' to 'Center (mode-2 node)'. How do the peak in the mode-2 window (the 0.7~1.4 $N_2$ speed range where the lab searches for the mode-2 peak) and the response near 3000 rpm change?
3. Turn on 'Overlay isolated modal contributions'. Check that the center's mode-2 contribution is zero.
4. Return to 'Both bearings' and change the mode-2 damping ratio from 0.04 to 0.20. Read the contribution peak and resultant phase together.

</Callout>

<MultiModeBodeLab client:visible lang="en" />

In step 2, the center has no local peak in the mode-2 window, so the readout is —. In step 3, the mode-2 amplitude is exactly 0. In step 4, the isolated mode-2 amplitude at 3000 rpm falls from 75 to 15 µm pp, but its phase transition remains, with a gentler slope.

## 4. Damping and mode spacing: which curve did you use for AF?

Does a peak reduced by more damping mean the same thing as two peaks merged by moving the modes closer together?

<Figure {...F.dampingOverlap} />

The upper plot in Figure 6 is the isolated mode-2 response: greater damping makes the peak lower and wider. The lower plot is the combined response with the modes closer together, strengthening vector superposition. Nearby modes affect each other's peak positions, half-power widths, and phase changes.

The $\mathrm{AF}=N_c/(N_2-N_1)$ in P4-1 is easier to interpret with an isolated peak and half-power crossings on both sides. In that formula, $N_c$ is the peak speed and $N_1$ and $N_2$ are the half-power crossing speeds; they are not the mode-numbered $N_m$ used on this page. For learning, the lab displays **AF of the isolated unbalance modal contribution**. Do not read it as a damping estimate from a measured combined curve containing bow, runout, and noise.

<Callout type="example" title="Distinguish the AF approximation from separation margin">

The half-power AF of the isolated first contribution, calculated at 2 rpm intervals, is about 8.215. It differs slightly from the small-damping approximation $1/(2\zeta_1)=8.333$.

The conceptual separation margins between operation at 3600 rpm and the sampled bearing-1 peaks at 1500 and 3020 rpm are $\lvert3600-1500\rvert/3600\times100=58.33\%$ and $\lvert3600-3020\rvert/3600\times100=16.11\%$, respectively. These values alone do not determine whether operation is allowed.

</Callout>

When the modes are close, the lab's two 0.7~1.4 $N_m$ windows can overlap and capture the same peak twice. Read them together with both bearings, the center sensor, and the isolated contributions. An actual review needs data matched to the design dynamic analysis and measurement conditions.

## 5. Run-up and coastdown: match thermal state and compensation reference

If startup and shutdown amplitudes differ at the same speed, can we immediately conclude that the machine has changed? First distinguish <strong>run-up</strong>, an acceleration record, from <strong>coastdown</strong>, a deceleration record. Then match the thermal state, load, and sensor references of the records.

<Figure {...F.thermalComparison} />

Figure 7 represents hot shaft bending, or <strong>thermal bow</strong>, by adding an equivalent initial displacement $b_1=2$ µm Peak·60° to mode 1. This bow simplifies bending fixed in rotating coordinates; it is not the result of a heat-transfer calculation. P8-2 continues with how thermal bow develops and changes.

The bow term passes through the same denominator as the unbalance term, but its numerator is not multiplied by $r_m^2$, so it does not shrink at low speed.

$$
X_j(N)=\sum_{m=1}^{2}\Phi_{jm}
\frac{e_m r_m^2e^{-j\theta_m}+b_m e^{-j\beta_m}}
{1-r_m^2+j2\zeta_m r_m}
$$

$b_m$ and $\beta_m$ are the equivalent thermal-bow magnitude and lag angle for the chosen shape normalization. As speed approaches 0, the unbalance term disappears while the bow term remains. The 0 rpm point in the figure is a mathematical limit, not a measurement of a rotating signal.

<Figure {...F.runoutEffect} />

As in Figure 8, constant runout is also added to the measured vector. Slow roll compensation from P3-3 subtracts a vector measured at low speed from the whole record, so it removes the actual dynamic response and bow at that instant along with runout. Keep the uncompensated record and compensation reference.

<Callout type="try">

1. The lab below starts with hot coastdown and bow of 2 µm Peak·60°. Read 200 rpm and the peak in the first window.
2. Turn on '200 rpm slow roll vector compensation'. Although 200 rpm becomes 0, does the 1500 rpm response match the cold case?
3. Change the thermal state to 'Cold run-up (bow 0)'. Turn compensation off, and toggle 'Add runout' to compare the low-speed curves.
4. Read Bode and Polar plots before and after compensation, remembering that the reference also removes dynamic response.

</Callout>

<MultiModeBodeLab initialHot={true} client:visible lang="en" />

In step 1, the hot response at 200 rpm is about 4.179 µm pp. In step 2, subtracting that vector makes 200 rpm zero, but changes the hot response at 1500 rpm only from 103.7 to 100.8 µm pp, still different from the cold 83.46 µm pp. **Constant-vector compensation cannot remove the entire speed-dependent bow response.**

<Callout type="warn" title="'Every run-up/coastdown difference is thermal bow?'">

Also check stiffness and bearing conditions, contact, load, and excitation changes associated with thermal state. Filter delay from P5-5 can shift the recorded peak speed differently during acceleration and deceleration. This lab calculates only the steady-state response at each speed, so it does not reproduce differences due to actual acceleration rate or filter delay.

</Callout>

## 6. Crossing critical speeds: what should you record as amplitude rises?

Residual unbalance and bow that excite a mode, together with changes in damping and support conditions, can increase amplitude near a critical speed. Bow and unbalance can add or cancel when their directions differ, so a hot response is not always larger. If you encounter a large peak, do not decide its cause from one sensor's magnitude alone.

| What to check | What to record | Related concepts |
|---|---|---|
| Which modal region is it? | rpm markers, 1X amplitude, phase, and Polar at each bearing | Figures 2~5 on this page, P4-1 |
| Are cold/hot state and load matched? | State before startup, temperature, load, startup/shutdown direction | Figure 7, P8-2 |
| Has measurement shifted the curve? | rpm interval, acceleration rate, filter settings, timestamps | P3-5, P5-5 |
| Is cancellation or runout involved? | Uncompensated record, slow roll rpm and reference vector | Figures 3 and 8, P3-3 |
| Are components other than 1X growing? | Direct amplitude, Waterfall, orbit | P6-1~P6-3 |

The reason to avoid unnecessary dwell and follow approved acceleration/deceleration procedures through the crossing region is that response can grow near resonance. This steady-state lab does not calculate 'how fast to cross for a given amplitude'. Actual crossing response depends on acceleration rate, damping, and initial state.

<Callout type="field" title="Revisit the trip multiplier as a protection function in P3-5">

A trip multiplier used during startup temporarily changes specified protection settings. It neither removes modal peaks nor automatically authorizes large vibration. Check its speed range, duration, return conditions, and actual protection-channel records against the approved procedure for the machine.

</Callout>

P7-2 covers distinguishing bow and unbalance in the 1X family; P7-3 covers rubbing contact between the shaft and stationary parts. This linear example cannot establish whether contact, a crack, or instability is present.

## 7. Summary

| What you read | What to remember |
|---|---|
| Modes and critical speeds | Several modes can meet 1X. Use the full shape to distinguish rigid-body and bending motion |
| Peaks and phase | Together they strengthen the case for resonance. Resultant phase need not change by 180° per mode |
| Polar | A path of 1X vectors at successive speeds. Distinguish it from an orbit and align rpm markers with Bode plots |
| Sensor position | A node or weak excitation participation can hide a mode. Check direction and Keyphasor reference too |
| AF and SM | Distinguish AF of an isolated contribution from a combined peak. SM here is a conceptual calculation |
| Startup and shutdown | Match thermal state, load, filter, and compensation reference. Bow and runout differ |
| Operating response | Check approved crossing and protection procedures together with raw records |

## 8. Check your understanding

<details>
<summary><strong>Q1. At 3000 rpm, the center sensor reads 19.94 µm pp while the bearings read about 75~77 µm pp. Is mode 2 absent?</strong></summary>

No. The center's mode-2 shape value is 0, so that sensor has no mode-2 contribution. The 19.94 µm pp is the high-speed tail of mode 1. Check other locations and the mode shape.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2. The bearings have a phase difference of 160° near mode 2. Can you conclude that it is not an anti-phase mode?</strong></summary>

No. The mode-2 contributions in this model have exactly opposite signs, but mode 1 also appears in the measured resultant. Compare contributions and several locations using the same direction and Keyphasor reference.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3. If the mode-2 damping ratio increases from 0.04 to 0.20, what is the isolated contribution's amplitude at N₂?</strong></summary>

Since $2e_2/(2\zeta_2)=e_2/\zeta_2$, it falls from $3/0.04=75$ µm pp to $3/0.20=15$ µm pp. The combined sensor response may differ because it includes other modes.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4. What is the conceptual SM for operation at 3600 rpm and a combined peak at 3020 rpm?</strong></summary>

It is $\lvert3600-3020\rvert/3600\times100=16.11\%$. This is neither a standard's requirement nor an operating-acceptance decision.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q5. Subtracting the hot 200 rpm vector makes the low-speed amplitude zero. Has thermal bow been removed?</strong></summary>

The reference sample becomes zero because it is subtracted from itself. At 1500 rpm, the compensated 100.8 µm pp still differs from the cold 83.46 µm pp. Read the speed-dependent bow response together with the uncompensated data.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q6. If startup and shutdown peaks appear at different speeds, what do you compare first?</strong></summary>

Match thermal state, load, sensor and phase references, slow roll compensation, acceleration rate, and filter settings. Filter delay alone can shift the recorded speed. Narrow down resonance, contact, and other candidates using several plots and operating records.
</details>
