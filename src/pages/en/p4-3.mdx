# 4-3 Where does the shaft sit on its film of oil?

Before start-up, the shaft center is below the bearing center. As you raise the speed, the shaft center climbs and also moves sideways. The vibration amplitude got smaller, yet the mean position has changed a lot. What do these two changes tell you?

In P4-2 we looked at the orbit that the shaft center traces during one revolution at a single section. This time we look at the **mean point** of that orbit. To read how the mean position moves, we first need to know how the oil film holds the shaft up.

<Callout type="idea" title="After reading this page">

1. You can tell apart the support from film pressure generated by rotation and the support from external pressurization.
2. You can express the mean shaft position with radial clearance, eccentricity ratio and attitude angle.
3. You can recover the shaft centerline from the DC voltages of two probes and a cold reference position.
4. You can sort the causes of a change in mean position into mechanical causes and measurement causes, and check them.
5. You can say which forces and motions the 8 stiffness and damping coefficients of the oil film connect.

</Callout>

---

## 1. Where does the film pressure come from?

In P1-6 §4 we said that a fluid-film bearing carries the load with a film of oil between the shaft and the bearing. The cylindrical part of the shaft that sits inside the bearing and is supported by it is the <strong>journal</strong>. If the journal sits lower than the bearing center, the clearance around it is not uniform.

The rotating journal surface drags oil along. When the oil goes from a wide place into a narrow one, viscosity builds up pressure. When the force obtained by adding up this pressure over the journal's circumference and length balances the load acting downward, the mean position at which the shaft floats is fixed.

<Figure {...F.oilWedge} />

In Figure 1, the upper curve is the clearance and the lower curve is the pressure. The place of maximum pressure and the place of minimum clearance are not the same (with θ measured from the maximum clearance in the spin direction, in this example the pressure peaks at θ ≈ 155° and the clearance is smallest at 180°). So the net force from the pressure is not exactly aligned with the line of centers either. That is why the shaft does not just rise straight up but also moves sideways.

<Callout type="term" title="Hydrodynamic and hydrostatic support">

<strong>Hydrodynamic</strong> support carries the load with pressure generated by relative motion and a narrowing clearance. <strong>Hydrostatic</strong> support carries the load with the pressure of an externally pressurized fluid. Jacking oil, which lifts the shaft at low speed or at rest, is an example of the latter. The mere fact that a pump supplies lubricating oil does not make it hydrostatic support.

</Callout>

The cylindrical bearing model on this page has no externally pressurized support. At 0 rpm it shows the cold reference point (the reference position taken while the machine is stopped and cool) where the shaft rests on the bottom. At low speed and high load the minimum film can thin to a few µm, which is comparable to the surface roughness and puts you in the mixed-lubrication regime; this model does not cover that regime. Nor is it a model for calculating the start-up procedure or the allowable contact conditions of real equipment.

## 2. Clearance, eccentricity ratio, attitude angle: three numbers for the position

Let the bearing center be O and the geometric center of the journal be C. The <strong>radial clearance</strong> $C_r$ is the difference between the bearing bore radius and the journal radius. The <strong>diametral clearance</strong>, the bore diameter minus the journal diameter, is $2C_r$.

<Figure {...F.clearance} />

The distance $e$ from O to C is the <strong>eccentricity</strong> at this section. The unbalance eccentricity e in P4-2 was the distance from the geometric center C of the shaft to its center of mass G. The e here is the distance <strong>between the bearing center and the journal center</strong>, so it is a different quantity.

The <strong>eccentricity ratio</strong> is the eccentricity divided by the radial clearance.

$$
\varepsilon=\frac{e}{C_r},\qquad h_{\min}=C_r-e=C_r(1-\varepsilon)
$$

$h_{\min}$ is the minimum film thickness for a cylindrical geometry. When concentric, ε = 0; when touching the bottom, ε = 1. For a rotating-film equilibrium we look for solutions with 0 < ε < 1.

The <strong>attitude angle</strong> φ is the angle from the line along which the load acts to OC. On this page, +X points right, +Y points up, and the load W points down. Viewed from the same side of the shaft end (the same viewing direction as P4-2), for counterclockwise spin the model puts C at the lower right, so

$$
x=C_r\varepsilon\sin\phi,\qquad y=-C_r\varepsilon\cos\phi
$$

For clockwise spin, flip the sign of x. φ is shown as a magnitude. When you read other figures, first check that the load direction, the viewing direction and the angle reference are the same.

<Callout type="example" title="If ε = 0.6758 at a radial clearance of 100 µm">

The eccentricity is 67.58 µm and the minimum film is 32.42 µm. If you divide e by the diametral clearance of 200 µm, you get ε = 0.3379, which is wrong by a factor of two. Nor is ε the ratio of the peak-to-peak vibration to the clearance.

</Callout>

## 3. Speed, load and viscosity move the equilibrium point

How strongly the oil resists flow is expressed by the <strong>dynamic viscosity</strong> μ, in Pa·s. In this model μ is held constant. With journal radius R, bearing length L and rotational angular speed Ω, the load relation of a short cylindrical bearing is

$$
\frac{WC_r^2}{\mu\Omega RL^3}
=\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{16\varepsilon^2+\pi^2(1-\varepsilon^2)}}{4(1-\varepsilon^2)^2}
$$

$$
\phi=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\pi\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^2}}{4\varepsilon}\right)
$$

The right side of the first equation grows as ε grows. If you keep W and raise Ω or μ, the left side gets smaller, so the required ε gets smaller too. Conversely, a larger load needs a larger eccentricity ratio. The attitude angle gets larger as ε gets smaller.

In bearing literature the same group is often written as the <strong>Sommerfeld number</strong> $S=(\mu N/P)(R/C_r)^2$ ($N$: revolutions per second, $P=W/(LD)$: load per projected area, $D=2R$). The left side above equals $1/(4\pi S(L/D)^2)$. When S is large (fast, light, or high viscosity), the shaft floats close to the center.

<Figure {...F.equilibrium} />

As Figure 3 shows, changing only the speed changes both the distance (eccentricity ratio) and the direction (attitude angle) of the journal center. If you draw it as "the shaft always rises straight up as speed increases", you miss this sideways motion.

<Callout type="warn" title="Whose solution is this?">

The example is a <strong>short bearing</strong> approximation with R = 50 mm, L = 25 mm and Cr = 100 µm. It assumes isothermal, constant-viscosity, incompressible, laminar flow, and uses the half-Sommerfeld condition, in which only the half where the clearance narrows is taken to have positive pressure and the other half is set to zero. It does not replace a precise analysis of a real finite-length bearing or of a <strong>tilting-pad</strong> bearing, whose pads tilt as they move.

</Callout>

## 4. The shaft centerline joins the mean points of the orbits

The <strong>shaft centerline</strong> is a plot of the mean shaft center position obtained at each speed or operating state, drawn relative to the bearing center. Unlike an orbit, which pairs two displacements taken at the same instant, each point here holds the <strong>mean position</strong> of one state.

<Figure {...F.centerline} />

In Figure 4, what moves along the blue line is the mean point at each speed. The center of the small green orbit lies on the blue line. As the mean point moves up, the orbit may get smaller; or the mean point may stay put while only the orbit gets bigger.

For the cold state we assume the shaft rests on the bottom and set $(x_{\rm cold},y_{\rm cold})=(0,-C_r)$. These coordinates are the <strong>cold position relative to the bearing center</strong>. In real equipment the reference position can differ with jacking oil, the contact position and the bearing geometry, so do not copy this assumption as it is.

<Callout type="field" title="A mean point does not fix a single cause">

If the shaft moves closer to the center at the same speed, the load may have decreased or the viscosity may have increased. A <strong>light load</strong>, where the load on the bearing is small, is a clue for checking dynamic stability (P4-4), but a small ε by itself is not a verdict of instability. You also have to look at the orbit, the vibration frequency and the operating conditions.

</Callout>

## 5. To turn two DC voltages into X and Y, you need a reference point

The proximity probe in P3-2 expressed the gap between the shaft surface and the probe as a voltage (§2). In that signal, the <strong>DC component</strong>, the average over some period, is linked to the mean gap (§3). The <strong>AC component</strong>, the deviation from the average, is linked to the vibration around that position.

Suppose probe A looks at the shaft from 45° and probe B from 135°, measured from +X. Even though the two probes are at right angles to each other, A and B are not the horizontal X and vertical Y.

<Figure {...F.probes} />

Writing the sensitivity magnitude as a positive S, the linear relation in this example is $V=-S d$, where d is the gap. When the shaft gets closer to the probe, the gap shrinks and the magnitude of the negative voltage shrinks. Subtracting the cold voltage and dividing by S gives the change q, which is how far the shaft has moved toward each probe:

$$
q_A=\frac{V_A-V_{A,\rm cold}}{S}=\frac{\Delta x+\Delta y}{\sqrt2},\quad
q_B=\frac{V_B-V_{B,\rm cold}}{S}=\frac{-\Delta x+\Delta y}{\sqrt2}
$$

$$
x=x_{\rm cold}+\frac{q_A-q_B}{\sqrt2},\qquad
y=y_{\rm cold}+\frac{q_A+q_B}{\sqrt2}
$$

Here Δx and Δy are the <strong>displacement from the cold position</strong>. The voltage difference alone gives only the displacement; you must add the cold coordinates to get the position relative to the bearing center. The projection of the gap is a first-order approximation for the case where the displacement is much smaller than the journal radius.

<Callout type="example" title="Check the voltages first">

At 3000 rpm, 1000 N and μ = 20 mPa·s, the model gives X = 43.97 µm and Y = −51.32 µm. With a cold gap of 1.20 mm and S = 7.874 V/mm, the cold voltage is −9.449 V, and the mean voltages in operation are A −8.933 V and B −9.423 V. A has come much closer, but the voltage of B also changes a little.

</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Compare the voltages and the mean position">

1. At the defaults of 3000 rpm, 1000 N and 20 mPa·s, check that ε = 0.6758 and the minimum film = 32.42 µm. The two position markers should overlap.
2. Lower the load to 500 N. You get ε = 0.5597 and a minimum film of 44.03 µm. Reset, then raise the speed to 6000 rpm and compare whether the static position is the same.
3. After a reset, turn off <strong>Add cold shaft position</strong>. The reconstructed position shifts up by 100 µm. Reset again and set the A voltage drift (a slow shift of the voltage after the cold record, §6) to +0.5 V to see an error of 63.5 µm.
4. After a reset, change the cold gap to 0.30 mm. The reconstructed point disappears because it is out of range. Reset and turn on only the zero-mean runout; the DC point stays.

</Callout>

<ShaftCenterlineLab client:visible lang="en" />

### What did you see?

If you halve the load, you get X = 42.44 µm and Y = −36.49 µm. The distance to the origin shrinks and the film gets thicker. Instead of halving the load, doubling the speed or the viscosity gives the same ε and φ, because W and μΩ enter the equation only as the group W/(μΩ). So from a single position you cannot back out the load or the viscosity separately.

The 100 µm error when the cold reference is left out equals the radial clearance Cr. When +0.5 V is put on A, an error of $0.5/S=63.5$ µm appears along the normal direction of A (the 45° direction from which probe A looks at the shaft). In both cases the blue oil-film equilibrium point stays where it was.

With a small installation gap, the gap at A drops below the linear range (P3-2 §4). If you keep drawing coordinates with the linear sensitivity then, you get a plausible wrong answer. The lab stops reconstructing the position and keeps the voltages and the true gaps.

## 6. Separate position change from measurement change

<Figure {...F.referenceErrors} />

If the probe zero, the sensitivity or the bracket position changes, or if a <strong>drift</strong> occurs, in which the voltage slowly shifts after the cold record was taken, the mean point looks like it moves even though the oil-film equilibrium is the same. A constant bias that is in both the cold and the operating records cancels in the voltage difference, but a change that arises between the records remains.

The runout of P3-2 §5 (a signal that appears from the surface condition even when the shaft is not moving) also has to be examined by separating mean from vibration. In the linear range, averaging a zero-mean sinusoidal runout over a whole number of revolutions gives a DC shift of zero. But for the runout value at the angle where the shaft happened to stop when the cold reference was recorded, for a surface deviation whose mean is not zero, for an average that does not fill a whole revolution, and in a nonlinear calibration region, you cannot apply the same conclusion directly.

<Callout type="field" title="Record the conditions along with a long-term trend">

At the same speed and a similar load, first check the voltage calibration and the reference point. Thermal expansion can change the housing and probe references and the clearance. A change in μ with temperature or a change in clearance from wear can affect the position. Misalignment (the shafts not lining up) can change the load on the bearing, and so can a design geometry that moves the center of curvature of pads or lobes (the several arc segments that make up the bearing bore), or a different external radial load. This static cylindrical model is not a diagnostic tool that tells these causes apart.

</Callout>

In this model, where the load is applied only downward, the mean point has $Y<0$. If the measured mean point is above the center, first check for other radial loads (misalignment, external load), and then the cold reference and the probe condition.

If the center has moved, rather than first concluding that "the oil film got worse", compare the sensor condition, speed, load, lube-oil temperature and start-up history on the same time axis. The step to check whether it is a dynamic problem is to look further at the orbit and the frequency components.

## 7. Around the equilibrium point, stiffness and damping become matrices

So far we have found only the mean point of the motion. As with the stiffness of P1-1 and the damping of P1-3, let us express the <strong>change in oil-film force</strong> when the shaft is moved slightly from that point. Write the small displacements at the equilibrium point as δx and δy, and the small velocities as δẋ and δẏ.

<Figure {...F.coefficients} />

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\delta F_x\\\delta F_y\end{bmatrix}
=-\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}k_{xx}&k_{xy}\\k_{yx}&k_{yy}\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{stiffness [N/m]}}
\begin{bmatrix}\delta x\\\delta y\end{bmatrix}
-\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}c_{xx}&c_{xy}\\c_{yx}&c_{yy}\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{damping [N·s/m]}}
\begin{bmatrix}\delta\dot x\\\delta\dot y\end{bmatrix}
$$

The first subscript is the force direction and the second is the displacement or velocity direction. $k_{xx}, k_{yy}$ and $c_{xx}, c_{yy}$ are the <strong>direct coefficients</strong>, which connect the same direction. $k_{xy}, k_{yx}$ and $c_{xy}, c_{yx}$ are the <strong>cross-coupled coefficients</strong>, which connect different directions.

For example, $k_{xy}$ is how a Y displacement affects the X force. In general it cannot be assumed equal to $k_{yx}$. The 4 stiffnesses and 4 dampings together are called the <strong>8 dynamic coefficients</strong>. This page covers only their meaning and does not calculate their values.

The fact that the mean position is the same does not fix the 8 coefficients or the stability of the rotor. The next page looks at what happens when these cross-direction forces feed energy into the whirl.

## 8. Relations to remember and review questions

| Question | Relation to remember |
|---|---|
| How does a rotating film carry the load? | The net force of the pressure built up as viscous fluid is dragged into a converging clearance |
| What is the denominator of the eccentricity ratio? | Radial clearance Cr. The diametral clearance is 2Cr |
| How does the mean position differ from the orbit? | The path of the mean points over states / the vibration around the mean point during one revolution |
| To turn DC voltage into coordinates? | Linear calibration, probe angles, cold voltage, and the cold position relative to the bearing center |
| Is there a single cause of a center shift? | Load, μ, clearance and sensor changes all contribute, so compare the conditions |
| What do the subscripts of the 8 coefficients mean? | First: force direction. Second: displacement or velocity direction |

<details>
<summary><strong>Q1. With a diametral clearance of 200 µm and e = 60 µm, what are ε and the minimum film?</strong></summary>

Since Cr = 100 µm, ε = 0.6 and the minimum film is 40 µm. It is not 0.3, which you get by using the diametral clearance as the denominator.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2. At 1000 N and 3000 rpm, changing only the load to 500 N gives a static position equal to which speed?</strong></summary>

It equals that of 1000 N at 6000 rpm, because in a short-bearing model with all else the same, W/(μΩ) is the same. That does not mean the dynamic stability of the two states is the same.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3. If the A voltage goes from −9.449 V to −8.933 V, did the gap increase?</strong></summary>

The gap decreased. With V = −S d, a voltage that is less negative means the surface has come closer. That displacement is a projection onto the A normal direction, so it is not equal to the X displacement.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4. We got Δx and Δy by subtracting the cold voltage, so why is y<sub>cold</sub> still needed?</strong></summary>

The difference gives only the displacement from the cold state. A position relative to the bearing center needs the cold coordinates. If you leave them out in the lab, Y shifts up by 100 µm.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q5. Can the orbit of one revolution get bigger while the DC mean point stays the same?</strong></summary>

Yes. A zero-mean vibration can change its AC size while keeping the DC position. Conversely, the mean point can move while the vibration stays small.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q6. Which displacement produces which force in the coefficient k<sub>yx</sub>?</strong></summary>

It is how an X displacement affects the Y force. In the sign convention used here, −k<sub>yx</sub>·δx enters δF<sub>y</sub>. The actual sign and size of the coefficient come from the dynamic analysis of the bearing.
</details>
